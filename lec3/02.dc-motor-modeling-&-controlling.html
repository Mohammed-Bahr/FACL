Part 2: Control

الفكرة الأساسية في Part 2: Control إننا مش عايزين نفهم الموتور بيشتغل إزاي بس إحنا عايزين نعرف إزاي نتحكم في سرعته واتجاهه، ونتأكد إنه وصل فعلًا للحركة المطلوبة.

Control means giving a command to a system so that its response matches the objective accurately. Our command knob is the voltage 𝑉. The question is how to set it from code.

Imagine you have a robot arm and you want it to move at an angular velocity of 30 rad/s.

You have:

  • Command: The desired speed.
  • Controller: The program or microcontroller that determines the drive signal.
  • Motor: The motor that executes the command.
  • Output: The actual speed the motor reaches.

The problem is that if you send a specific voltage to the motor, it won't necessarily reach the exact desired speed because of load, friction, and voltage fluctuations.

Therefore, we have 3 fundamental topics:

  1. PWM: How do we control the power delivered to the motor?
  2. H-Bridge & Encoders: How do we control direction and measure motion?
  3. Open-loop & Closed-loop (in the next note ان شاء الله): How do we make the motor execute the command accurately?
PWM sets how much power the motor gets → the H-bridge sets which way it spins → an encoder measures what actually happened → closed-loop control compares that with what you wanted and corrects.

2.1 PWM (Pulse Width Modulation)

Intuition: instead of turning a tap halfway, flick it fully on and off so quickly that the downstream flow averages out.

  • During𝑇ON: switch closed → full battery voltage across the motor.
  • During𝑇OFF: switch open → motor electrically disconnected.

Total cycle time And the Duty Cycle ratio: 𝑇=𝑇𝑂𝑁+𝑇𝑂𝐹𝐹,𝛼=𝑇𝑂𝑁𝑇

  • 𝛼 =1 gives full speed, 𝛼 =0 stops, and anything between gives proportional speed.

If the duty cycle is 40%, it means the voltage is connected for 40% of each cycle.

  • With 𝑉𝐵 =12 V and 𝛼 =0.25, the average is 3 V.
  • Speed follows the average voltage: 𝜔𝑎𝑣𝑔 ≈𝛼,𝜔𝑚𝑎𝑥. For example, 0.4 ×100 =40 rad/s.
    Why the motor "sees" only the average: the switching period (about 1 ms, say) is much shorter than the motor's mechanical time constant (tens of ms).
    The inertia and the coil inductance smooth the pulses out.

Average voltage:
𝑉𝑎𝑣𝑔=1𝑇∫𝑇𝑜𝑛0𝑉𝐵⋅𝑑𝑡=𝑇𝑜𝑛𝑇𝑜𝑛+𝑇𝑜𝑓𝑓𝑉𝐵friction, load, winding resistance and battery sag all shift the real speed. So 𝜔𝑎𝑣𝑔≈𝛼𝜔𝑚𝑎𝑥is a first approximation. That is also why closed loop is needed later.

Practical problems

The problems

Problem 1: inductive kickback.

  • The inductor voltage is 𝐿,𝑑𝑖/𝑑𝑡. It is positive at the rising edge.
  • At the falling edge the current is cut abruptly, so 𝑑𝑖/𝑑𝑡 is hugely negative. The inductor acts as a large reverse voltage source. The notes call this "back EMF"; don't confuse it with 𝐾𝑡𝜔.
  • This causes sparking and destroys the switch.
  • Fix: flywheel (flyback) diode across the motor.
    • Rising edge: the diode is reverse-biased, so it acts as an open circuit and does nothing.
    • Falling edge: the inductor's reversed polarity forward-biases it, so the current circulates through the motor–diode loop and decays safely as heat.

Problem 2: radio interference. The PWM frequency can be close to the radio band, so the motor radiates noise. Fix: shield the motor.

Problem 3: direction control. A single switch can only spin the motor one way. The fix is the H-bridge, which uses four switches.

With SW1 and SW2 on the top side, and SW3 and SW4 on the bottom:

ON Result
SW1 + SW4 current left→right, motor spins one way
SW2 + SW3 current right→left, spins the other way
SW1 + SW3 (or SW2 + SW4) forbidden: shoots through from supply to ground (short circuit)
SW1 + SW2 brake (per your notes)
SW3 + SW4 "stop by inertia" (per your notes)

I'd double-check the last two rows with your instructor. Physically, shorting both motor terminals together (both top or both bottom) brakes the motor hard, because the motor's own voltage drives current through the short. Coasting (free stopping by inertia) is when all four switches are off.

2.2 H-bridge: choosing the direction



Reverse the current in the armature and the motor reverses. Four switches do this:

ON Result
SW1 + SW4 current one way → spins one way
SW2 + SW3 current reversed → spins the other way
SW1 + SW3, or SW2 + SW4 shoot-through: supply shorted to ground, destroys the switches
SW1 + SW2, or SW3 + SW4 both motor terminals tied together → brake
all OFF coast, spins down slowly through friction
  • Dead time is a tiny pause that driver circuits insert when swapping a top switch for a bottom one, so both are never on at once.
  • Brake vs coast: braking creates a path that opposes the motion, so it stops fast. Coasting just disconnects the motor.
  • Note: this table is more physically accurate than your notes. Your notes list SW3 + SW4 as "stop by inertia", but shorting both terminals to ground is a brake. Coasting is all-off.

Kinematics = the geometry of motion, ignoring what causes it like power or torque

  • It ignores forces, torques, and accelerations. Only position and velocity exist in this world.
  • Anything about "will the battery handle this?", "how much torque does the motor need?", or energy belongs to dynamics, a different course topic.
افترض إن عندك ذراع روبوت طوله مترين.

في الـ Kinematics، تقدر تحسب مكان إيده لما تغيّر زوايا المفاصل، حتى لو مش عارف نوع الموتور المستخدم.
لكن في الـ Dynamics، لما تطلب منه يشيل حمولة وزنها (5,kg)، محتاج تدرس العزوم والكتل والقصور الذاتي عشان تعرف هل الموتور يقدر ينفذ الحركة المطلوبة.

It splits into two parts:

  1. Position kinematics: It relates joint angles and positions to the location and orientation of the robot's end effector. This is the core part we will be working on today.(This lecture)
  2. Differential (velocity) kinematics: This examines the relationship between joint velocities and the velocity of the robot's end-effector, often utilizing the Jacobian. This is an extension of the current lecture the detailed derivation is not covered here. (Next lecture)

    Why ignore forces? A robot arm is a chain of rigid links. Where its hand ends up depends only on the shape of the chain (link lengths and joint angles), not on how hard the motors pushed. So geometry alone answers "where is the hand?".

Forward and inverse kinematics: 𝑝=𝑓(𝑞)(forward)𝑞=𝑓−1(𝑝)(inverse)

  • ~={blue}Forward kinematics=~ (~={green}FK=~): "I know the joint angles. Where is the hand or end factor?" Maps Configuration Space→Task Space
  • ~={blue}Inverse kinematics=~ (~={green}IK=~): "I want the hand there. What joint angles do I need?" Maps: Task Space→Configuration Space
  • Why both matter in a real robot: the motors are commanded in joint space (~={brown}you send them angles=~), but you think and plan in task space ("~={brown}grab the cup at this position=~").
    ~={green}IK converts your goal into motor commands=~. ~={yellow}FK converts motor readings back into "where is my hand now?",=~ which is needed for feedback.
This is why the lecture opened with PID control the controller drives each joint, and kinematics tells it which joint values to aim for.

The two spaces: configuration & task

Configuration 𝑞:

"how the robot is set up"

Take a planar arm with rotating joints. Knowing as 𝜃1 and 𝜃2⋯𝜃𝑛 fully determines the arm's shape: 𝑞=⎡⎢ ⎢ ⎢ ⎢ ⎢ ⎢⎣𝜃1𝜃2⋮𝜃𝑛⎤⎥ ⎥ ⎥ ⎥ ⎥ ⎥⎦

  • 𝑞 is the Configuration Vector its contains joint position vector, with 𝑛 entries (one per joint).
  • 𝜃1 is angle measured from the ground/x-axis to link 1.
  • 𝜃2 is angle measured from the extension of Previous link, which is link 1 to link 2. It is a relative angle, not measured from the ground.

مثلًا:
𝑞=[30∘60∘]معناه إن المفصل الأول عند 30∘، والمفصل التاني عند 60∘، وفقًا لتعريف الزوايا اللي اتفقنا عليه.

Task position 𝑝:

"where the hand is, in the world"

Position Matrix: 𝑝 describes the end effector (the tool or hand) and has 𝑚 entries.

  • In 3D, a rigid body has at most 6 DOF: 3 translations (𝑥,𝑦,𝑧) and 3 rotations (𝜙𝑥,𝜙𝑦,𝜙𝑧). So 𝑚 ≤6. 𝑝=⎡⎢ ⎢ ⎢ ⎢ ⎢ ⎢ ⎢ ⎢ ⎢ ⎢⎣𝑥𝑦𝑧𝜙𝑥𝜙𝑦𝜙𝑧⎤⎥ ⎥ ⎥ ⎥ ⎥ ⎥ ⎥ ⎥ ⎥ ⎥⎦
  • In 2D, at most 3: (𝑥,𝑦) plus one rotation 𝜙. So 𝑚 ≤3. →𝑝 =⎡⎢ ⎢⎣𝑥𝑦𝜙⎤⎥ ⎥⎦
The lecture gave useful examples of choosing 𝑚 based on the task:
  • A car on a road: 𝑚 =3 at most (position and heading).
  • A train on a rail: 𝑚 =1 (distance along the track).
  • A ball you want to move: 𝑚 =2 in 2D, because a ball has no "front", so orientation is irrelevant. You only track (𝑥,𝑦).
You choose 𝑝 to contain only what the task cares about.

The two spaces

  • Configuration space = all possible values of 𝑞 in the space.
  • Task space = all possible values of 𝑝 in the space.

Concrete picture from the lecture: if 𝜃1,𝜃2 ∈[0,2𝜋), the configuration space is a square, and one point in the square is one pose of the robot. (Strictly, since 0 and 2𝜋 are the same angle, it wraps around like a donut, a torus. A square is a fine first picture.)

Forward kinematics

Forward Kinematics (𝐹𝐾) calculates end-effector pose from known joint angles (𝑋 =𝑇(𝑞)), Inverse Kinematics solves the reverse mapping (𝑞 =𝑓−1(𝑋)).

It simply means how to calculate Task Vector from the Configuration Vector i have.

Consider two links of lengths 𝐿1 and 𝐿2, connected by revolute joints. Let:

  • 𝜃1 be the absolute angle of link 1 from the 𝑥-axis.
  • 𝜃2 be the relative angle of link 2 with respect to link 1.
From Right triangle with hypotenuse 𝑙1:
  • Horizontal projection → 𝑥 =𝑙cos⁡𝜃.
  • Vertical Projection → 𝑦 =𝑙sin⁡𝜃.
  1. Calculate the end of the first link:

تخيّل إنك أسقطت نهاية الجزء الأول على المحورين 𝑋 و𝑌.
من المثلث القائم: cos⁡𝜃1=𝑥1𝑙1إذن:𝑥1=𝑙1cos⁡𝜃1وبنفس الطريقة:sin⁡𝜃1=𝑦1𝑙1إذن:𝑦1=𝑙1sin⁡𝜃1كده عرفنا مكان المفصل التاني، لأنه موجود عند نهاية الجزء الأول.
لاحظ إن الـ Cosine بيجيب الإسقاط الأفقي، والـ Sine بيجيب الإسقاط الرأسي، لأن الزاوية مقاسة من المحور الأفقي.

  1. Calculate the end of the second Link:

الجزء الثاني طوله 𝑙2، واتجاهه بالنسبة للمحور الأفقي هو:

𝜃1+𝜃2

إذن الإسقاط الأفقي للجزء الثاني:

𝑥2=𝑙2cos⁡(𝜃1+𝜃2)

والإسقاط الرأسي:

𝑦2=𝑙2sin⁡(𝜃1+𝜃2)

خد بالك إننا استخدمنا مجموع الزاويتين، مش 𝜃2 لوحدها؛ لأن 𝜃2 زاوية نسبية بين الوصلتين.

  1. Then the End Effector Coordinates is

نهاية الذراع موجودة بعد ما نمشي الجزء الأول، وبعده الجزء التاني.
وبالتالي الموقع النهائي بيساوي مجموع الإسقاطات:𝑥=𝑥1+𝑥2نعوّض: 𝑥=𝑙1cos⁡𝜃1+𝑙2cos⁡(𝜃1+𝜃2)وبالنسبة للمحور الرأسي: 𝑦=𝑦1+𝑦2إذن:𝑦=𝑙1sin⁡𝜃1+𝑙2sin⁡(𝜃1+𝜃2)دي أول معادلتين أساسيتين في المحاضرة.
وآخر حاجة، اتجاه الجزء الأخير هو: 𝜙=𝜃1+𝜃2

So Forward Kinematics Equations is: (for 2D as now)

𝑝=𝑓(𝑞)=⎡⎢ ⎢⎣𝑥𝑦𝜙⎤⎥ ⎥⎦=⎡⎢ ⎢⎣𝐿1cos⁡𝜃1+𝐿2cos⁡(𝜃1+𝜃2)𝐿1sin⁡𝜃1+𝐿2sin⁡(𝜃1+𝜃2)𝜃1+𝜃2⎤⎥ ⎥⎦

Key property: FK always gives exactly one answer. Plug in angles, get a position. It is just evaluating a formula, which is why the lecturer called it easy.

Inverse kinematics

Inverse Kinematics (𝐼𝐾) is a core problem in robotics that deals with finding the joint variables (angles for rotation joints, extensions for prismatic joints) required to place a robot's end effector at a desired position and orientation in task space.

What is the end of the robot domain before even start just by math:

Let’s think about this geometrically. We have two links with lengths 𝑙1 and 𝑙2. The maximum possible distance between the robot's base and the end of the arm occurs when both links are aligned in a straight line, pointing in the same direction: 𝑟max=𝑙1+𝑙2The minimum distance, on the other hand, occurs when the two links are directed toward each other in this case, the distance between the end of the arm and the base is the absolute difference between the two lengths: 𝑟min=|𝑙1−𝑙2|Therefore, in an ideal model assuming no additional constraints on the joints—the condition for reaching a point with coordinates (𝑥,𝑦) is:
|𝑙1−𝑙2|≤√𝑥2+𝑦2≤𝑙1+𝑙2This is because the straight-line distance between the arm's base and the target is:
𝑟=√𝑥2+𝑦2Of course, if there are mechanical limits on the joint angles or obstacles in the workspace, a point might fall within these geometric bounds yet still be practically unreachable.

An important point to note: in this lecture, the basic Inverse Kinematics problem deals only with 𝑥 and 𝑦 coordinates the orientation of the gripper is not necessarily specified. This is allowed because of the possibilities of multiple solutions for the same target position.

The Law of Cosines states that for any triangle with sides 𝑎,𝑏, and 𝑐, where 𝜃 is the angle between sides 𝑎 and 𝑏:𝑐2=𝑎2+𝑏2−2𝑎𝑏⋅cos⁡𝜃
Where does this law come from? (full derivation) click to unfold

Setup: place the vertex with angle 𝐶 (the angle between sides 𝑎 and 𝑏) at the origin. Lay side 𝑎 flat along the x-axis: its far end is the point 𝐵 =(𝑎, 0). Side 𝑏 rises from the origin at angle 𝐶: its far end is the point 𝐴 =(𝑏cos⁡𝐶, 𝑏sin⁡𝐶).

Step 1: side 𝑐 is just the straight-line distance between 𝐴 and 𝐵, so apply the distance formula:
𝑐2=(𝑎−𝑏cos⁡𝐶)2+(0−𝑏sin⁡𝐶)2

Step 2: expand both squares:
𝑐2=𝑎2−2𝑎𝑏cos⁡𝐶+𝑏2cos2⁡𝐶+𝑏2sin2⁡𝐶

Step 3: factor out 𝑏2 and use the identity cos2⁡𝐶 +sin2⁡𝐶 =1:
𝑐2=𝑎2−2𝑎𝑏cos⁡𝐶+𝑏2(cos2⁡𝐶+sin2⁡𝐶)=1

Conclusion:
𝑐2=𝑎2+𝑏2−2𝑎𝑏cos⁡𝐶

Note what happened: the term −2𝑎𝑏cos⁡𝐶 appeared the moment we squared (𝑎 −𝑏cos⁡𝐶). And since 𝑏cos⁡𝐶 and 𝑏sin⁡𝐶 are the projections of side 𝑏 onto side 𝑎, geometrically we dropped an altitude of height ℎ =𝑏sin⁡𝐶 and applied Pythagoras on the two right triangles it creates the Law of Cosines is really "Pythagoras with a correction term" for triangles that aren't right-angled.

Sanity check: if 𝐶 =90 °, then cos⁡𝐶 =0 and the law collapses to 𝑐2 =𝑎2 +𝑏2 Pythagoras itself. ✓

In our triangle, the interior angle between 𝑙1 and the line 𝑟 is supplementary to the joint angle 𝜃2 (i.e., they add up to 180∘). If we denote the interior angle as 𝜓:𝜓=𝜋−𝜃2

Substituting:cos⁡(𝜋−𝜃2)=−cos⁡𝜃2

Step 1: find 𝜃2

Let 𝜓 be the interior angle at the elbow مِرفَق (opposite side 𝑟):
𝑟2=𝑙21+𝑙22−2𝑙1𝑙2cos⁡𝜓⇒cos⁡𝜓=𝑙21+𝑙22−𝑟22𝑙1𝑙2

But 𝜃2 is measured from link 1's extension, so it's the exterior angle. They are supplementary: 𝜓 +𝜃2 =𝜋. Hence

𝜃2=𝜋−cos−1⁡(𝑙21+𝑙22−𝑥2−𝑦22𝑙1𝑙2)

Step 1 in (full detail)

0. Draw the triangle: base at origin 𝑂, elbow at 𝐸, target at 𝑇. The three sides are:

  • 𝑂𝐸 =𝑙1 (link 1)
  • 𝐸𝑇 =𝑙2 (link 2)
  • 𝑂𝑇 =𝑟 (straight line from base to target)

The interior angle at the elbow 𝐸 is 𝜓, and it faces side 𝑟 (it is opposite to 𝑟).

1. Express 𝑟 from the target: the distance from the base to the target (𝑥,𝑦) is
𝑟2=𝑥2+𝑦2

2. Apply the Law of Cosines: in the triangle, side 𝑟 is opposite the angle 𝜓 (the angle between sides 𝑙1 and 𝑙2), so:
𝑟2=𝑙21+𝑙22−2𝑙1𝑙2cos⁡𝜓

3. Solve for cos⁡𝜓: move −2𝑙1𝑙2cos⁡𝜓 to the left and 𝑟2 to the right, then divide by 2𝑙1𝑙2:
2𝑙1𝑙2cos⁡𝜓=𝑙21+𝑙22−𝑟2⇒cos⁡𝜓=𝑙21+𝑙22−𝑟22𝑙1𝑙2

4. Substitute 𝑟2 =𝑥2 +𝑦2: replace 𝑟 with what we know from the target coordinates:
cos⁡𝜓=𝑙21+𝑙22−(𝑥2+𝑦2)2𝑙1𝑙2⇒𝜓=cos−1⁡!(𝑙21+𝑙22−𝑥2−𝑦22𝑙1𝑙2)

5. Convert 𝜓 →𝜃2: 𝜃2 is not the interior angle it is measured from link 1's extension (continuation straight past the elbow). So 𝜃2 is the exterior angle at the elbow. Interior + exterior always sum to a straight line:
𝜓+𝜃2=𝜋⇒𝜃2=𝜋−𝜓Using cos⁡(𝜋 −𝜃2) = −cos⁡𝜃2, the same result can be written without the 𝜋 − as:
cos⁡𝜃2=𝑥2+𝑦2−𝑙21−𝑙222𝑙1𝑙2⇒𝜃2=cos−1⁡!(𝑥2+𝑦2−𝑙21−𝑙222𝑙1𝑙2)

6. Domain check (reachability): cos−1 only accepts values in [ −1,1]. The argument 𝑙21+𝑙22−𝑟22𝑙1𝑙2 being outside that range means no triangle can be formed the target is outside the reachable ring |𝑙1 −𝑙2| ≤𝑟 ≤𝑙1 +𝑙2.

Sanity checks:

  • 𝜓 =90 °: cos⁡𝜓 =0, so 𝑟2 =𝑙21 +𝑙22. That is exactly Pythagoras. ✓
  • 𝜓 =180 °: arm fully stretched, so 𝜃2 =0 and 𝑟 =𝑙1 +𝑙2. ✓

Step 2: find 𝜃1

𝜃1 is the angle of link 1 above the x-axis. The target direction has angle tan−1⁡(𝑦/𝑥). Link 1 sits below that line by an angle 𝛽 (the triangle's interior angle at the origin), so:
𝜃1=tan−1⁡𝑦𝑥−𝛽,𝛽=cos−1⁡(𝑙21+𝑟2−𝑙222𝑙1𝑟)

(𝛽 comes from the law of cosines again, now for the side 𝑙2 opposite it.) Combined:

𝜃1=tan−1⁡𝑦𝑥−cos−1⁡!(𝑙21+𝑥2+𝑦2−𝑙222𝑙1√𝑥2+𝑦2)

Step 2 in (full detail)

0. What we know: the same triangle 𝑂–𝐸–𝑇 with sides 𝑙1, 𝑙2, 𝑟 =√𝑥2+𝑦2. We already have 𝜃2 from Step 1; now we need the whole arm's tilt.

1. Identify 𝛽: 𝛽 is the interior angle at the origin 𝑂 the angle between link 1 (side 𝑙1) and the line to the target (side 𝑟). It faces side 𝑙2, i.e. 𝑙2 is the side opposite 𝛽.

2. Apply the Law of Cosines this time for side 𝑙2: the law is symmetric; here the side opposite the angle 𝛽 is 𝑙2, and the two sides touching 𝛽 are 𝑙1 and 𝑟:
𝑙22=𝑙21+𝑟2−2𝑙1𝑟cos⁡𝛽

3. Solve for cos⁡𝛽: same algebra as before (move, then divide):
cos⁡𝛽=𝑙21+𝑟2−𝑙222𝑙1𝑟⇒𝛽=cos−1⁡!(𝑙21+𝑟2−𝑙222𝑙1𝑟)

4. Substitute 𝑟 =√𝑥2+𝑦2: 𝑟 is not a "given", it comes from the target:
𝛽=cos−1⁡!(𝑙21+(𝑥2+𝑦2)−𝑙222𝑙1√𝑥2+𝑦2)

5. Get the target's direction: the straight line from base to target makes an angle with the x-axis of
𝛼=tan−1⁡𝑦𝑥

6. Assemble 𝜃1: look at the geometry link 1 does not point at the target. It points below that line, because the elbow bends back down (or the target line is above). The triangle at 𝑂 tells us link 1 sits at angle 𝛽 below the target line, so we subtract:
𝜃1=𝛼−𝛽

7. Combine 4 + 5 + 6:
𝜃1=tan−1⁡𝑦𝑥−cos−1⁡!(𝑙21+𝑥2+𝑦2−𝑙222𝑙1√𝑥2+𝑦2)

Quadrant warning: tan−1⁡(𝑦/𝑥) only reads the ratio (𝑥,𝑦) and ( −𝑥, −𝑦) give the same answer. In code always use atan2(y, x).

مثال نطبّق الـ Forward Kinematics

افترض إن عندك القيم دي:

𝑙1=2𝑚,𝑙2=1𝑚

𝜃1=30∘,𝜃2=60∘

𝑥=2cos⁡30∘+1cos⁡90∘

وإحنا عارفين إن:

إذن:

𝑥=2(√32)+0

𝑥≈1.732𝑚

𝑦=2sin⁡30∘+1sin⁡90∘

وبما إن:

يبقى:

𝑦=2(12)+1(1)

𝑦=2𝑚

وأخيرًا:

𝜙=90∘

إذن وضع نهاية الروبوت هو:

𝑝=⎡⎢ ⎢⎣1.732290∘⎤⎥ ⎥⎦

يعني لو حرّكنا المفصلين للقيم دي، فإيد الروبوت هتبقى عند 𝑥 ≈1.732 𝑚، و𝑦 =2 𝑚، واتجاهها رأسي لأعلى.

مثال كامل على الـ Inverse Kinematics

تعالَ نحل مثالًا بالأرقام ونراجع الناتج بالـ Forward Kinematics.

عندنا ذراع بطول:

𝑙1=1𝑚,𝑙2=1𝑚

والهدف موجود عند:

المطلوب: نوجد 𝜃1,𝜃2.

الخطوة الأولى: نحسب المسافة

الخطوة الثانية: نحسب 𝜃2

الحل الأول

نراجع الحل الأول باستخدام Forward Kinematics

عندنا:𝑙1=𝑙2=1,𝜃1=0∘,𝜃2=90∘نحسب:
𝑥=cos⁡0∘+cos⁡90∘=1+0=1𝑦=sin⁡0∘+sin⁡90∘=0+1=1إذن وصلنا فعلًا إلى الهدف:
(𝑥,𝑦)=(1,1)وبالنسبة للحل الثاني:
𝜃1=90∘,𝜃2=−90∘يبقى الاتجاه المطلق للوصلة الثانية:
90∘−90∘=0∘وبالتالي:
𝑥=cos⁡90∘+cos⁡0∘=0+1=1𝑦=sin⁡90∘+sin⁡0∘=1+0=1نفس النتيجة.
الدرس هنا: الـ Forward Kinematics بتديك مكان النهاية من وضع المفاصل، لكن معرفة مكان النهاية وحده مش بالضرورة تكفي لتحديد وضع المفاصل بشكل وحيد.

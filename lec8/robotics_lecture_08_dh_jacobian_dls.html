1. The physical picture & where the frames go (T ≈ 0:02–3:40)

Recap (mentioned last lecture, not re-explained): the Denavit–Hartenberg (DH) convention is a general method for computing the transformation matrix between frames.

The robot on the board:

  • a cylinder = a revolute joint think of it as a motor rotating by an angle 𝜃1;
  • a second motor rotating by 𝜃2.

Frame placement, exactly as stated:

  1. The base coordinate system {0} =(𝑥0,𝑦0,𝑧0) is placed at the first joint (DH style).
  2. The next frame {1} =(𝑥1,𝑦1,𝑧1) is placed at the end of the link that connects joint 1 and joint 2 and these axes belong to joint 1.
  3. Rule: a joint always rotates about its 𝑧-axis ⇒ 𝑧0 is the axis that joint 1 rotates about. The joint's own (current) frame sits attached on top of it.
  4. The frame attached to joint 1 rotates with it relative to the world (it moves when 𝜃1 moves), while {0} is the fixed world frame.
  5. Rotation about 𝑧 is measured from 𝑥 toward 𝑦 right-hand rule: thumb points along +𝑧.
  6. The last frame {2} =(𝑥2,𝑦2,𝑧2) is not attached to a joint it belongs to the end effector.

2. Which frame "owns" which matrix (T ≈ 3:41–6:06)

Schematic of a robot in a general position: a frame {𝑛 −1} =(𝑥𝑛−1,𝑦𝑛−1,𝑧𝑛−1) sits at the joint being discussed, and the end of the link carries {𝑛} =(𝑥𝑛,𝑦𝑛,𝑧𝑛).

The rule he stressed (this is the one that prevents all index mistakes):

The coordinate system drawn at a joint belongs to what comes before it "the joint's coordinate system is specific to what is before the joint."

  • 𝜃𝑛 is the rotation about 𝑧𝑛−1, from 𝑥𝑛−1 toward 𝑦𝑛−1 the same angle as the motor/joint angle.
  • His remark on the 𝑥-axis: the axes at the end of the link belong to the current joint, but the 𝑥 direction is the one along which the next joint's motion happens (it "serves" the next rotation).
  • If another joint comes after, then 𝑧𝑛 is that joint's rotation axis.

3. The four DH parameters (T ≈ 6:45–10:08)

Picture two planes: the plane of {𝑛}, i.e. (𝑥𝑛,𝑦𝑛), raised above the plane of {𝑛 −1}, i.e. (𝑥𝑛−1,𝑦𝑛−1). Like a table and a mobile phone on it: the phone's plane does not have to be parallel to the table it can be tilted a little. That tilt is exactly what 𝛼 captures (the 𝑧 axis, being the rotation axis perpendicular to the plane, tilts with the plane).

Parameter Definition from the lecture Physical meaning
𝜃 rotation about 𝑧𝑛−1, from 𝑥𝑛−1 toward 𝑦𝑛−1 the joint angle (same as the motor angle)
𝛼 the tilt between the two 𝑥𝑦-planes = angle between 𝑧𝑛−1 and 𝑧𝑛 link twist
𝑟 the perpendicular distance between the axes (𝑥𝑛−1,𝑧𝑛−1) and (𝑥𝑛,𝑧𝑛), measured along the 𝑥 direction becomes the link length when a link exists
𝑑 the height of the (𝑥𝑛,𝑦𝑛) plane above the (𝑥𝑛−1,𝑦𝑛−1) plane, measured along 𝑧 link offset

He flagged this twice ("خلي بالنا" pay attention): measure 𝑟 along 𝑥 and 𝑑 along 𝑧. Mixing them up is the classic DH error.

4. Deriving the general DH matrix (T ≈ 10:14–15:41) THE core result

Recap: the matrix has a rotation part (the cos/sin business done last lecture) and a translation part. Here they are assembled into one matrix.

Why this construction? 𝜃 is defined about 𝑧, and 𝛼 (the twist between the planes) is taken about 𝑥. So the rotation block must be:

Rot(𝑧,𝜃)on the LEFT⋅Rot(𝑥,𝛼)on the RIGHT

His memorization trick (he said it explicitly this is only a way to remember it, not a new derivation): "rotation about 𝑥 goes on the right, rotation about 𝑧 goes on the left."

Rot(𝑧,𝜃)=⎡⎢ ⎢⎣cos⁡𝜃−sin⁡𝜃0sin⁡𝜃cos⁡𝜃0001⎤⎥ ⎥⎦,Rot(𝑥,𝛼)=⎡⎢ ⎢⎣1000cos⁡𝛼−sin⁡𝛼0sin⁡𝛼cos⁡𝛼⎤⎥ ⎥⎦

Multiplying (only these two matrices "don't overthink the rest, it is just multiplication"):

Rot(𝑧,𝜃)Rot(𝑥,𝛼)=⎡⎢ ⎢⎣cos⁡𝜃−sin⁡𝜃cos⁡𝛼sin⁡𝜃sin⁡𝛼sin⁡𝜃cos⁡𝜃cos⁡𝛼−cos⁡𝜃sin⁡𝛼0sin⁡𝛼cos⁡𝛼⎤⎥ ⎥⎦

The translation column why it looks like this: the offset 𝑟 lies along 𝑥; rotating the vector (𝑟,0,0) by 𝜃 about 𝑧 gives (𝑟cos⁡𝜃, 𝑟sin⁡𝜃, 0); the offset 𝑑 is added along 𝑧. Total: (𝑟cos⁡𝜃, 𝑟sin⁡𝜃, 𝑑). [extra two-line justification of the column he simply stated]

Bottom row [ 0  0  0  1 ] the homogeneous-form convention.

The DH matrix (exactly as on handwritten page 1):

𝐴𝑖−1𝑖=⎡⎢ ⎢ ⎢ ⎢ ⎢⎣cos⁡𝜃−sin⁡𝜃cos⁡𝛼sin⁡𝜃sin⁡𝛼𝑟cos⁡𝜃sin⁡𝜃cos⁡𝜃cos⁡𝛼−sin⁡𝛼cos⁡𝜃𝑟sin⁡𝜃0sin⁡𝛼cos⁡𝛼𝑑0001⎤⎥ ⎥ ⎥ ⎥ ⎥⎦

5. Notation (T ≈ 15:57–17:00)

This DH matrix is named 𝐴 one matrix per coordinate frame number 𝑖. It transforms from frame 𝑖 −1 to frame 𝑖, so we can write it either way (same thing):

𝐴𝑖−1𝑖 ≡ 𝑖−1𝑇𝑖

6. Back to the 2R example: the frames (T ≈ 17:06–20:23)

Recap: this is the simple example from before link 1 of length 𝑙1 at angle 𝜃1, link 2 of length 𝑙2 at angle 𝜃2 measured from the extension of link 1.

Frame placement on it (the sketch on handwritten page 1):

  • {0} at the first joint = the world frame. 𝑧0 is perpendicular to the page, pointing outward because the plane of 𝑧 is the normal to the 𝑥𝑦-plane, and taking 𝑥0 →𝑦0 with the right-hand rule points 𝑧0 outward. In a planar (2D) problem we never draw 𝑧: it is always perpendicular to the plane (in or out but perpendicular).
  • {1} at the end of link 1 (= at joint 2): 𝑧1 is the rotation axis of joint 2 also perpendicular to the page, hence not visible.
  • 𝑥1: a general rule take 𝑥 in the direction you want/need (here: along the link).
  • End effector: it must get its own frame, with the origin exactly at the end-effector point (as agreed previously). 𝑥𝑡 along the link (its working direction), 𝑧 outward.

7. Building 𝐴1 + verification (T ≈ 20:32–24:49)

We want 𝐴01: the transform from {0} to {1}. Read the parameters off the geometry:

  • 𝛼 =0 ⇒cos⁡𝛼 =1, sin⁡𝛼 =0 the two planes are parallel;
  • 𝑑 =0 both origins lie on the same plane, so there is no perpendicular distance between the two 𝑥𝑦-planes;
  • 𝑟 =𝑙1 (the link), 𝜃 =𝜃1 (the joint variable).

𝐴01=⎡⎢ ⎢ ⎢ ⎢ ⎢⎣cos⁡𝜃1−sin⁡𝜃10𝑙1cos⁡𝜃1sin⁡𝜃1cos⁡𝜃10𝑙1sin⁡𝜃100100001⎤⎥ ⎥ ⎥ ⎥ ⎥⎦

Verification (he did it to convince us the matrix is honest). Multiply 𝐴01 by the origin of frame {1} which is the end of the link, 𝑝 =⎡⎢ ⎢ ⎢ ⎢ ⎢⎣0001⎤⎥ ⎥ ⎥ ⎥ ⎥⎦.

Mechanically, every entry of the product is

(column 1)⋅0+(column 2)⋅0+(column 3)⋅0+(column 4)⋅1

i.e. all columns vanish except the 4th. So:

𝐴01𝑝=⎡⎢ ⎢ ⎢ ⎢ ⎢⎣𝑙1cos⁡𝜃1𝑙1sin⁡𝜃101⎤⎥ ⎥ ⎥ ⎥ ⎥⎦

which is exactly the link-tip position (𝑙1cos⁡𝜃1, 𝑙1sin⁡𝜃1) the geometry checks out. ✔

Two extraction rules to remember (used again later)

𝑇⎡⎢ ⎢ ⎢ ⎢ ⎢⎣0001⎤⎥ ⎥ ⎥ ⎥ ⎥⎦=4th column=position of the frame origin,𝑇⎡⎢ ⎢ ⎢ ⎢ ⎢⎣0010⎤⎥ ⎥ ⎥ ⎥ ⎥⎦=3rd column=direction of the frame’s 𝑧-axis

9. Chaining: 𝐴02 =𝐴01𝐴12 by blocks (T ≈ 26:37–36:07)

Why multiply? The end effector is reached from the world by two hops: {0} →{1} then {1} →{2}. Composing transforms = multiplying their matrices.

The trick he used (and recommended): partition each 4 ×4 into four blocks a 3 ×3 rotation 𝑅𝑖, a 3 ×1 translation 𝑡𝑖, a zero row, and the corner 1:

𝐴𝑖=[𝑅𝑖𝑡𝑖031],𝑡𝑖=⎡⎢ ⎢⎣𝑙𝑖cos⁡𝜃𝑖𝑙𝑖sin⁡𝜃𝑖0⎤⎥ ⎥⎦

Then multiply as if they were 2 ×2 block matrices:

𝐴01𝐴12=[𝑅1𝑡1031][𝑅2𝑡2031]=[𝑅1𝑅2𝑅1𝑡2+𝑡1031]

("All we need is 𝑅1𝑅2 and 𝑅1𝑡2 +𝑡1" the zero blocks kill everything else.)

Step 1 𝑅1𝑅2. Both are pure 𝑧-rotations, by 𝜃1 then by 𝜃2 ⇒ the product is a 𝑧-rotation by the sum. ("Rotating by 𝜃1 about 𝑧 then by 𝜃2 about 𝑧 = one rotation of 𝜃1 +𝜃2. Anyone not convinced: multiply it out you get the same thing.") Using the angle-sum identities:

cos⁡𝜃1cos⁡𝜃2−sin⁡𝜃1sin⁡𝜃2=cos⁡(𝜃1+𝜃2),sin⁡𝜃1cos⁡𝜃2+cos⁡𝜃1sin⁡𝜃2=sin⁡(𝜃1+𝜃2)

𝑅1𝑅2=⎡⎢ ⎢⎣𝑐12−𝑠120𝑠12𝑐120001⎤⎥ ⎥⎦

Step 2 𝑅1𝑡2 +𝑡1. Row by row (this is the expansion written on handwritten page 1):

𝑅1𝑡2+𝑡1=⎡⎢ ⎢⎣cos⁡𝜃1−sin⁡𝜃10sin⁡𝜃1cos⁡𝜃10001⎤⎥ ⎥⎦⎡⎢ ⎢⎣𝑙2cos⁡𝜃2𝑙2sin⁡𝜃20⎤⎥ ⎥⎦+⎡⎢ ⎢⎣𝑙1cos⁡𝜃1𝑙1sin⁡𝜃10⎤⎥ ⎥⎦

  • Row 1: 𝑙2(cos⁡𝜃1cos⁡𝜃2 −sin⁡𝜃1sin⁡𝜃2) +𝑙1cos⁡𝜃1 =𝑙2cos⁡(𝜃1 +𝜃2) +𝑙1cos⁡𝜃1
  • Row 2: 𝑙2(sin⁡𝜃1cos⁡𝜃2 +cos⁡𝜃1sin⁡𝜃2) +𝑙1sin⁡𝜃1 =𝑙2sin⁡(𝜃1 +𝜃2) +𝑙1sin⁡𝜃1
  • Row 3: 0

The result (handwritten page 1):

𝐴02=⎡⎢ ⎢ ⎢ ⎢ ⎢⎣cos⁡(𝜃1+𝜃2)−sin⁡(𝜃1+𝜃2)0𝑙1cos⁡𝜃1+𝑙2cos⁡(𝜃1+𝜃2)sin⁡(𝜃1+𝜃2)cos⁡(𝜃1+𝜃2)0𝑙1sin⁡𝜃1+𝑙2sin⁡(𝜃1+𝜃2)00100001⎤⎥ ⎥ ⎥ ⎥ ⎥⎦

10. Reading the answers out of the matrix (T ≈ 36:00–40:40)

The end-effector frame was chosen with its origin at the tool and 𝑥 along its working direction convenient, because the end effector "sees" points relative to itself.

Where is the end effector? Multiply the big matrix by the world origin the origin gets carried through the chain (moved into {1}, then into {2}), so in the end the transformed world origin is the end-effector position:

𝑝𝑒=𝐴02⎡⎢ ⎢ ⎢ ⎢ ⎢⎣0001⎤⎥ ⎥ ⎥ ⎥ ⎥⎦=4th column of 𝐴02=⎡⎢ ⎢ ⎢ ⎢ ⎢⎣𝑙1cos⁡𝜃1+𝑙2cos⁡(𝜃1+𝜃2)𝑙1sin⁡𝜃1+𝑙2sin⁡(𝜃1+𝜃2)01⎤⎥ ⎥ ⎥ ⎥ ⎥⎦

(these exact lines are on handwritten pages 1–2). The scalar part,

𝑥=𝑙1cos⁡𝜃1+𝑙2cos⁡(𝜃1+𝜃2),𝑦=𝑙1sin⁡𝜃1+𝑙2sin⁡(𝜃1+𝜃2),𝑧=0

is the forward-kinematics equation the relation between end-effector location and joint angles, and it came straight out of the 4th column.

Where does the 𝑧-axis of the end effector point? Multiply by ⎡⎢ ⎢ ⎢ ⎢ ⎢⎣0010⎤⎥ ⎥ ⎥ ⎥ ⎥⎦ ⇒ the 3rd column. Fully systematic this is exactly the kind of operation a computer runs.

11. The general DH recipe (T ≈ 40:46–43:30)

Why DH exists at all: to be programmed. Nobody computes these by hand in practice you analyze the robot once, and the rest runs as code.

The rules, as stated:

  1. For every joint, create a coordinate frame.
  2. 𝑧-axis:
    • revolute joint ⇒ 𝑧 = the rotation axis;
    • prismatic joint ⇒ 𝑧 = the direction of motion.
      (This is the fundamental rule of the whole convention.)
  3. 𝑥-axis: always perpendicular to 𝑧; preferred direction = along the common normal between the two consecutive 𝑧 axes.
    • Common normal, his explanation: given two lines (two 𝑧 axes), find the direction perpendicular to both extend/skew until the connecting segment is at 90∘ to both lines. That direction is what 𝑥 prefers.
  4. 𝑦-axis: completes the right-handed frame (right-hand rule).
  5. Then the parameters: 𝜃 = rotation angle about 𝑧; 𝛼 = tilt of the 𝑥𝑦-planes (angle between the 𝑧 axes); 𝑟 = link length; 𝑑 = distance along 𝑧.

12. DH table for the 3-joint robot: 2 revolute + 1 prismatic (T ≈ 43:36–50:22)

The robot in the figure has three joints: two drawn as cylinders = revolute, one drawn as a rectangle that extends up/down = prismatic ("something strange that goes out and comes back"). Frames per the recipe: revolute ⇒ 𝑧 = rotation axis; prismatic ⇒ 𝑧 = the up/down motion direction; 𝑥 = common normal between consecutive 𝑧's; 𝑦 = right-hand rule.

How the table was filled, joint by joint (the reasoning he said out loud):

  • Joint 1 (revolute): 𝜃 =𝜃1 (variable). 𝛼 =0 the two planes are parallel (a tilt would have shown up as a rotation about 𝑥). 𝑟 = the distance between the two origins along 𝑥. 𝑑 = the height of the plane above the previous one (along 𝑧) the distance between the two planes.
  • Joint 2 (revolute): filled the same way. 𝑟 = the perpendicular distance between the two 𝑧 axes (the length needed to reach the common normal), taken along 𝑥; 𝑑 = its offset along 𝑧.
  • Joint 3 (prismatic):
    • 𝜃 =0 a prismatic joint has no rotation at all;
    • 𝛼 =0;
    • 𝑟 = distance between the two 𝑧 axes along 𝑥 (here both 𝑧's point in the same direction);
    • 𝑑 is not constant: part of the link is fixed and part moves with the joint, so
      𝑑=𝑑fixed+𝑑3where 𝑑3 is the extension the joint variable (the value up to the current position).

14. Jacobian recap by direct differentiation (T ≈ 51:01–54:40)

Recap: last lecture we defined the Jacobian as the matrix of partial derivatives. He rewrote it quickly so we can compare it with the geometric method later.

Start from the FK equations we just extracted:

𝑥=𝑙1cos⁡𝜃1+𝑙2cos⁡(𝜃1+𝜃2),𝑦=𝑙1sin⁡𝜃1+𝑙2sin⁡(𝜃1+𝜃2)

Differentiate 𝑥 once w.r.t. 𝜃1 and once w.r.t. 𝜃2; same for 𝑦 (chain rule: 𝜕𝜕𝜃1cos⁡(𝜃1 +𝜃2) = −sin⁡(𝜃1 +𝜃2), and the 𝑙1 term carries no 𝜃2):

𝐽=[−𝑙1sin⁡𝜃1−𝑙2sin⁡(𝜃1+𝜃2)−𝑙2sin⁡(𝜃1+𝜃2)𝑙1cos⁡𝜃1+𝑙2cos⁡(𝜃1+𝜃2)𝑙2cos⁡(𝜃1+𝜃2)]

The two columns are labeled (as on handwritten page 2) 𝜕𝜕𝜃1 and 𝜕𝜕𝜃2:

𝐽1=[−𝑙1sin⁡𝜃1−𝑙2sin⁡(𝜃1+𝜃2)𝑙1cos⁡𝜃1+𝑙2cos⁡(𝜃1+𝜃2)],𝐽2=[−𝑙2sin⁡(𝜃1+𝜃2)𝑙2cos⁡(𝜃1+𝜃2)]

(the margin note on the page: 𝑣 = linear, 𝜔 = rotational).

15. Jacobian structure in general (T ≈ 54:40–59:27)

  • Number of columns = number of joints. 2 joints ⇒ 2 columns; 5 joints ⇒ 5 columns; 20 ⇒ 20.
  • In 3D, each column has 6 elements 3 for linear motion + 3 for rotational ⇒ 6 rows.
  • Column 𝑖 is called 𝐽𝑖 the Jacobian column of joint 𝑖.
  • Each column splits into two 3 ×1 vectors (handwritten page 2):

𝐽𝑖=[𝐽𝑣𝑖𝐽𝜔𝑖],𝐽𝑣𝑖 is 3×1 (linear component),𝐽𝜔𝑖 is 3×1 (rotational component)

so each 𝐽𝑖 is a 6-element vector: 3 entries in 𝐽𝑣𝑖, 3 entries in 𝐽𝜔𝑖.

16. Geometric Jacobian formulas (T ≈ 59:27–1:03:09)

The DH/textbook people gave closed formulas for 𝐽𝑣𝑖 and 𝐽𝜔𝑖 that can be programmed directly no symbolic differentiation needed. The form depends on the joint type (so: first check the joint revolute or prismatic then apply the matching formula).

Revolute joint 𝑖:

𝐽𝑣𝑖=𝑧𝑖−1×(𝑂𝑒−𝑂𝑖−1),𝐽𝜔𝑖=𝑧𝑖−1

where (keep the indices straight everything refers to the frame before the joint):

  • 𝑂𝑒 = origin of the end-effector frame (its position),
  • 𝑂𝑖−1 = origin of the frame before the joint,
  • 𝑧𝑖−1 = the 𝑧-axis of that previous frame.

His recipe in words: take the position of the end-effector origin, subtract the origin of the frame before the joint, take the 𝑧-axis of the frame before the joint, and cross-multiply.

[extra] Why 𝑧𝑖−1 ×(𝑂𝑒 −𝑂𝑖−1) makes sense

A point at position 𝑝 rigidly attached to a body rotating with angular velocity 𝜔 about an axis through point 𝑂 moves with velocity 𝜔 ×(𝑝 −𝑂). Here the axis direction is 𝑧𝑖−1 and the moment arm is (𝑂𝑒 −𝑂𝑖−1) the formula is exactly 𝜔 ×𝑟 with 𝜔 =𝑧𝑖−1.

Prismatic joint 𝑖:

𝐽𝑣𝑖=𝑧𝑖−1,𝐽𝜔𝑖=⎡⎢ ⎢⎣000⎤⎥ ⎥⎦

(a sliding joint translates the tool along 𝑧𝑖−1 but contributes no rotation).

17. Computing 𝐽1 for our robot (T ≈ 1:03:28–1:10:39)

First Joint, 𝑖 =1 (handwritten page 3). Collect the ingredients:

  • 𝑂𝑒 the end-effector origin, which we already have: it is the 4th column of 𝐴02:

𝑂𝑒=⎡⎢ ⎢⎣𝑙1cos⁡𝜃1+𝑙2cos⁡(𝜃1+𝜃2)𝑙1sin⁡𝜃1+𝑙2sin⁡(𝜃1+𝜃2)0⎤⎥ ⎥⎦

  • 𝑂0 origin of frame {0} = the world origin (for 𝑖 =1, the frame before the joint is {0}):

𝑂0=⎡⎢ ⎢⎣000⎤⎥ ⎥⎦,𝑧0=⎡⎢ ⎢⎣001⎤⎥ ⎥⎦

(𝑧0 is the unit vector 𝐤 the robot is planar, so the rotation axis is vertical.)

Cross product via the determinant (his method: put 𝐢,𝐣,𝐤 in the first row, 𝑧0 in the second, (𝑂𝑒 −𝑂0) in the third):

𝐽𝑣1=𝑧0×(𝑂𝑒−𝑂0)=∣ ∣ ∣ ∣ ∣ ∣ ∣ ∣𝐢𝐣𝐤001𝑙1cos⁡𝜃1+𝑙2cos⁡(𝜃1+𝜃2)𝑙1sin⁡𝜃1+𝑙2sin⁡(𝜃1+𝜃2)0∣ ∣ ∣ ∣ ∣ ∣ ∣ ∣

Expand along the first row:

  • 𝐢: +(0 ⋅0 −1 ⋅(𝑙1sin⁡𝜃1 +𝑙2sin⁡(𝜃1 +𝜃2))) = −(𝑙1sin⁡𝜃1 +𝑙2sin⁡(𝜃1 +𝜃2)) in the 𝑥 direction ✔
  • 𝐣: −(0 ⋅0 −1 ⋅(𝑙1cos⁡𝜃1 +𝑙2cos⁡(𝜃1 +𝜃2))) = +(𝑙1cos⁡𝜃1 +𝑙2cos⁡(𝜃1 +𝜃2)) the minus of the minus gives plus
  • 𝐤: 0

𝐽1=⎡⎢ ⎢⎣−𝑙1sin⁡𝜃1−𝑙2sin⁡(𝜃1+𝜃2)𝑙1cos⁡𝜃1+𝑙2cos⁡(𝜃1+𝜃2)0⎤⎥ ⎥⎦

Exactly what differentiation gave us in §14 ("as we got it before"). And 𝐽𝜔1 =𝑧0 =⎡⎢ ⎢⎣001⎤⎥ ⎥⎦.

18. Computing 𝐽2 (T ≈ 1:10:51–1:16:29)

For 𝑖 =2 we need the origin of the frame before joint 2, i.e. 𝑂1 = the end of link 1. Where does it come from? From the last column of 𝐴01 this is why we kept the matrices around:

𝑧1=⎡⎢ ⎢⎣001⎤⎥ ⎥⎦,𝑂1=⎡⎢ ⎢⎣𝑙1cos⁡𝜃1𝑙1sin⁡𝜃10⎤⎥ ⎥⎦

(planar robot ⇒ 𝑧1 is again 𝐤; if the robot were not planar, you would extract each 𝑧𝑖−1 from its transformation matrix multiply the matrix by ⎡⎢ ⎢ ⎢ ⎢ ⎢⎣0010⎤⎥ ⎥ ⎥ ⎥ ⎥⎦ and read the 3rd column.)

Subtract:

𝑂𝑒−𝑂1=⎡⎢ ⎢⎣𝑙2cos⁡(𝜃1+𝜃2)𝑙2sin⁡(𝜃1+𝜃2)0⎤⎥ ⎥⎦

(the 𝑙1 terms cancel geometrically: from joint 2 outward, only link 2 contributes).

𝐽𝑣2=𝑧1×(𝑂𝑒−𝑂1)=∣ ∣ ∣ ∣ ∣ ∣ ∣ ∣𝐢𝐣𝐤001𝑙2cos⁡(𝜃1+𝜃2)𝑙2sin⁡(𝜃1+𝜃2)0∣ ∣ ∣ ∣ ∣ ∣ ∣ ∣=⎡⎢ ⎢ ⎢⎣−𝑙2sin⁡(𝜃1+𝜃2)−(−𝑙2cos⁡(𝜃1+𝜃2))0⎤⎥ ⎥ ⎥⎦

𝐽2=⎡⎢ ⎢⎣−𝑙2sin⁡(𝜃1+𝜃2)𝑙2cos⁡(𝜃1+𝜃2)0⎤⎥ ⎥⎦,𝐽𝜔2=𝑧1=⎡⎢ ⎢⎣001⎤⎥ ⎥⎦

(matches handwritten page 3, and matches §14 again.)

19. Assembling the full Jacobian + its meaning (T ≈ 1:16:29–1:19:54)

Stack the linear parts on top and the angular parts below (handwritten page 4):

𝐽=⎡⎢ ⎢ ⎢ ⎢ ⎢ ⎢ ⎢ ⎢ ⎢ ⎢⎣−𝑙1sin⁡𝜃1−𝑙2sin⁡(𝜃1+𝜃2)−𝑙2sin⁡(𝜃1+𝜃2)𝑙1cos⁡𝜃1+𝑙2cos⁡(𝜃1+𝜃2)𝑙2cos⁡(𝜃1+𝜃2)00000011⎤⎥ ⎥ ⎥ ⎥ ⎥ ⎥ ⎥ ⎥ ⎥ ⎥⎦

Angular rows check: 0 rotational about 𝑥, 0 about 𝑦, 1 about 𝑧 for both columns (planar robot: rotation happens only about 𝑧).

And the mapping it performs (page 4):

˙𝑝=𝐽˙𝑞=𝐽[˙𝜃1˙𝜃2]

What the last equation means. An object in 3D always has position + orientation = POSE.

  • The top 3 rows × joint velocities ⇒ the position change of the end effector.
  • The bottom 3 rows × joint velocities ⇒ the orientation change. Multiplying the last row by [Δ𝜃1Δ𝜃2]𝑇 gives

Δ𝜙=Δ𝜃1+Δ𝜃2

i.e. the end-effector orientation change is the sum of the joint-angle changes which is visually obvious for this arm.

He closed the topic with: this procedure is what gets used in software such as ROS.

20. Inverse kinematics & the singularity problem (T ≈ 1:19:54–1:21:22)

Recap: last lecture we solved IK by inverting the Jacobian. For a general (non-square) 𝐽 that inversion goes through the pseudo-inverse route:

𝐽+=𝐽𝑇(𝐽𝐽𝑇)−1

The problem (his exact concern): 𝐽𝐽𝑇 is a square matrix but if it comes out singular, a problem occurs in the computations (the inverse does not exist). The Jacobian itself is not the issue a computer programs it easily; the inversion is the problem (singular configurations of the robot).

21. Damped Least Squares (DLS) (T ≈ 1:21:22–1:25:13)

An old method with a known derivation; the idea itself is simple. (The same damping idea is known in the literature as the Levenberg–Marquardt method.) [extra the alias only]

The fix: take the pseudo-inverse and add a parameter 𝜆 specifically 𝜆2 times the identity matrix:

𝐽+DLS=𝐽𝑇(𝐽𝐽𝑇+𝜆2𝐼)−1

Constraint stated in the lecture: 𝜆 must be positive.

Why this escapes the singularity: if 𝐽𝐽𝑇 is singular (determinant =0), adding a diagonal matrix with the positive value 𝜆2 on its diagonal makes the sum a positive matrix (positive definite) ⇒ no longer singular ⇒ invertible. In his words: he runs away from singularity by adding a factor into the computations.

Where 𝜆 comes from (the derivation route he mentioned): reformulate the problem as an optimization problem. Instead of minimizing only the 2-norm-squared residual ‖Δ𝑥 −𝐽 Δ𝑞‖2, also penalize the size of the joint change:

minΔ𝑞  ∥ ∥ ∥ ∥Δ𝑥−𝐽Δ𝑞∥ ∥ ∥ ∥2+𝜆2∥ ∥ ∥ ∥Δ𝑞∥ ∥ ∥ ∥2

— "add 𝜆2, the square of the value that comes out in Δ𝑞, and solve." Solving this yields the damped inverse above.

Choosing 𝜆 the trade-off he analyzed:

𝜆 Motion Precision
large smooth (he stressed: "he puts a large value ⇒ the change becomes smooth") low accuracy suffers
small closer to the raw inverse high, but the singularity problem we started with comes back

The application he showed: a 3D robot whose IK is solved with DLS, programmed to draw a circle and you notice it does not draw a perfect circle. That imperfection is a direct result of the damping introduced in the computations (DLS returns approximate, damped solutions).

22. Wrap-up & next steps (T ≈ 1:25:13–end)

  • With this lecture, kinematics + differential kinematics are finished.
  • Next (planned after the exams): a software called Webots a simulator in which we program the robot, mostly using the kinematics we just built.
  • Robots are programmed with an operating system called ROS (Robot Operating System): like Windows, but for robots except that instead of kernel functions, it is a set of nodes that send messages to each other.
  • Our job in ROS: write the main code that runs the robot to perform a mission e.g. grasp an object and move it somewhere else.
  • How motion is handled: the motion happens in steps control commands move position, move position, move position… and each step has a duration that depends on the robot's speed (a well-built, fast robot ⇒ small duration).
  • Workflow in the course: we build a simulation for specific trajectories on the computer (Webots), and once it succeeds we transfer it to the real robot. (Back in the day this was done by writing C++ inside ROS.)
